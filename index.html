So 60–75 g is a good target, but don't jump straight there.

You're going for double the UK minimum, which is closer to how humans ate for most of history. The one rule the book hammers is "low and slow": build up over about 8 weeks (the plan is in the Ramp-up tab), or your gut will make you regret it.

You're feeding a whole ecosystem

About 39 trillion microbes live in your colon, mostly bacteria, and every bite you eat ends up feeding them. They help run your immune system (70% of it sits next to your gut), metabolism, hormones, mood and how your genes get switched on. Diet changes which microbes thrive within 24 hours.

David et al., Nature 2014: switching to an all-animal diet changed the gut microbiome within a day.

Fibre is microbe food, not just roughage

Humans have about 17 enzymes for breaking down complex carbs. Your gut microbes have up to ~60,000. So you can't digest fibre yourself. It passes untouched to your colon, and your bacteria do the work.

The payoff is short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)

When bacteria ferment fibre they make acetate, propionate and butyrate. Butyrate supplies up to 70% of the energy for the cells lining your colon. It tightens the gut wall ("leaky gut"), calms inflammation, steadies blood sugar, triggers the hormones that tell you you're full, and reaches the brain. In the book's words: prebiotics (fibre) + probiotics (good microbes) = postbiotics (SCFAs).

Starve them and they eat you

On a low-fibre diet, gut bacteria switch to eating the protective mucus layer of your gut lining. The book's line is that two weeks of low fibre starts eroding the barrier. Fibre works like a trained skill: the more you eat regularly, the better your microbes get at turning it into SCFAs. Stop eating it and they lose the knack.

Desai et al., Cell 2016 (in mice).

Variety beats grams: aim for 30 plants a week

The book's "Golden Rule". The American Gut Project found that eating 30+ different plants a week was the strongest predictor of a diverse gut, more than whether someone was vegan, vegetarian or omnivore. Every plant carries a different mix of fibre, which feeds a different team of microbes. Herbs, spices, seeds, grains and beans all count.

McDonald et al., mSystems 2018.

Gas and bloating mean your gut is untrained, not inflamed

Struggling with beans or onions usually means your microbes aren't used to them yet. It doesn't mean the food is harming you. Treat your gut like a muscle: start low, go slow, and keep training it. Cutting food groups out makes the sensitivity worse over time. (A true allergy, with hives or swelling, is different. Avoid that food and see a doctor.)

Whole grains and beans are the foundation

Beans and lentils are the only food group that consistently predicted living longer across the world's long-lived populations. Whole grains lower inflammation markers. Unless you have coeliac disease, going gluten-free reduces good bacteria. Many people who think gluten bothers them are actually reacting to fructans (a FODMAP). The case against lectins rests on test-tube studies, while human studies keep finding that beans are good for you.

Skodje et al., Gastroenterology 2018: fructans, not gluten, triggered symptoms.

The "second meal effect"

Eat lentils at lunch and your blood sugar spikes less at dinner, even if dinner is white bread. The microbes you fed at lunch are still producing SCFAs hours later. Every fibre-rich meal sets up the next one.

Have a little fermented food every day

Sauerkraut, kimchi, miso, tempeh, live dairy-free yoghurt and proper sourdough bring in live microbes plus compounds made during fermentation. Start with a forkful, not a bowl. Kombucha is fine but it's not a cure-all, so don't down it by the litre.

A 2021 Stanford trial (after the book came out) found that fermented foods increased gut diversity in 10 weeks, which high fibre alone didn't yet do. Doing both makes sense.

It's the whole lifestyle, not only food

Give your gut about 13 hours off overnight (for example, finish eating by 8pm and eat again at 9am), with meals in daylight hours. Sleep, movement (start with 30-minute walks), stress, time with people and water all affect your microbes. Fibre needs water to move through you.

Where the book goes further than the evidence

A lot of the mechanisms come from mouse or test-tube studies, which the author sometimes treats as settled. His positions on dairy, organic food and "eat as much as you want and stay lean" are stronger than the research supports. The core message holds up well, though: eat more fibre from many different plants, and less ultra-processed food.

What to cut, what to limit, what to stop worrying about

The book's view, ranked by how hard it pushes. Some of these (dairy, nuts) you already avoid.

Cut

Ultra-processed food

Crisps, packaged snacks, ready meals, processed meat, fizzy drinks.

Every 10% more ultra-processed food in the diet was linked to about 10% higher cancer risk and 14% higher risk of dying early.

Cut

Refined carbs and added sugar

White bread, white pasta, sugary cereal, pastries, sweets.

With the fibre stripped out, they're absorbed before reaching your microbes, and they feed the inflammatory ones.

Cut

Artificial sweeteners

Diet drinks, "zero sugar" products, sweetener sachets.

Shown to shift gut bacteria towards glucose intolerance (Suez 2014). The author's own words: "Keep that crap away from me."

Cut

Certain additives

Check labels for carboxymethylcellulose (E466), polysorbate 80 (E433), trehalose, titanium dioxide (E171).

Emulsifiers caused gut inflammation in mice. Trehalose has been linked to more aggressive C. diff strains.

Cut

Trans fats and fried food

Margarine, deep-fried takeaway, anything listing "partially hydrogenated" oil.

Everyone agrees these are harmful.

Limit

Red and processed meat

The author aims for 90%+ plants, with meat as a side or a treat.

Gut bacteria turn compounds in red meat into TMAO, which is linked to heart disease. It also feeds inflammatory bacteria. Levels drop about 4 weeks after cutting back.

Limit

Saturated fat

Butter, ghee, coconut oil in coffee, fatty meat. Use olive oil instead.

In a 6-month trial, more fat in the diet shifted gut bacteria towards an inflammatory profile.

Limit

Fruit juice and smoothies

Eat whole fruit instead.

An orange has 2.3 g of fibre. A glass of OJ has 0.5 g and 2.5× the sugar.

Limit

Salt

Mostly hidden in processed food.

Excess salt changes gut bacteria in ways linked to autoimmunity and high blood pressure.

Limit

Medicines you don't need

Antibiotics, ibuprofen and other NSAIDs, PPIs (heartburn tablets).

5 days of ciprofloxacin wipes out about a third of gut bacteria. Don't stop anything prescribed. Just ask your GP whether you really need it.

Limit

Keto, carnivore, paleo

Fast weight loss, poor for your gut.

An all-animal diet grew inflammatory bacteria (Bilophila) within days. Paleo raised TMAO, mainly because it cuts out whole grains.

Stop worrying

Carbs, fruit, gluten, lectins, soy

Whole-food carbs, whole fruit, wholewheat, beans and soy (tofu, tempeh, edamame, miso) are all good for your gut.

The exception is gluten if you have coeliac disease or a wheat allergy.

Ramp up over 8 weeks

Jumping from ~20 g to 70 g overnight is how people end up bloated and give up. Move up a level every two weeks. If a level feels rough, stay on it longer. Tap a level to set today's target.

  • Add about 5 g every 3–4 daysFor example, swap white bread for wholemeal, then add chia to your oats, then add half a tin of beans.
  • Drink more water as you goAim for 2–2.5 L a day. Lots of fibre without enough water causes constipation.
  • Spread it across the dayThree meals of about 20 g each are easier on your gut than one 60 g dinner.
  • Start with the gentlest foodsRinsed tinned beans and lentils, red lentils, tofu, tempeh and oats. Raw onion, garlic and big bowls of beans come later.
  • Bloated? Hold, don't quitStay at that level for a week, then carry on. Some gas is normal. It's a sign your microbes are working.
  • Count plants as well as gramsAim for 30 different plants a week. Herbs, spices and seeds count, so a spice rack does a lot of the work.
  • Make sure you're not constipated firstThe book says constipation is the number one cause of bloating. Fix that before ramping up.
  • See a GP if……you notice blood in your poo, ongoing pain, unexplained weight loss, or a lasting change in how often you go.
